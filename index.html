About me

I am a seismologist interested in how rocky planets evolve through tectonic, volcanic, and internal processes. My research combines terrestrial seismic observations and planetary mission data with numerical seismic waveform simulations and inverse methods to investigate seismic activity, tectonics, and the structure and dynamics of planetary interiors.

Current interests include marsquakes and moonquakes, seismic source characterisation, seismic imaging, and the tectonic, volcanic, and deep-mantle dynamics of Earth and other rocky worlds.

Research stories

A closer look at a few questions behind the papers.

GRL · 2026

Can a single seismometer find a moonquake through the Moon’s seismic echoes?

Moonquake signals are strongly scattered, long thought to obscure their direction of travel. By tracking subtle changes in wave polarization across time and frequency, we recovered where the waves came from — and used that direction to locate moonquakes with a single Apollo station.

Nature Communications · Accepted

How can cold Mars still be tectonically active?

On cold Mars, a tectonic marsquake beneath Valles Marineris — the Solar System’s largest canyon — shows that billion-year-old faults can still rupture today.

Science Advances · 2026

What lies at the root of the Hawai'i plume?

Nearly 3,000 km beneath Hawai‘i, at the base of its mantle plume, lies a mysterious region where seismic waves slow dramatically. By combining seismology with mineral physics, we revealed the likely composition of this hidden structure at the core–mantle boundary.

Nature Communications · 2026

What happened to the Pacific plate 600 km beneath Northeast Asia?

The giant Pacific plate has sunk beneath East Asia and now lies stalled around 600 km deep. Seismic imaging reveals that this buried slab is broken by a ~1000-km-long gap, tracing ~30 million years of progressive tearing. The gap is not anomalously hot, challenging the common idea that slab gaps are windows for hot mantle upwelling.

JGR Solid Earth · 2018

How did a young volcanic island rise in the middle of a tectonic plate — and what still lies beneath it?

Jeju — home to UNESCO-listed volcanic landscapes — sits far from a conventional plate boundary. Seismic imaging reveals the deep structure that fed its volcanism, from sublithospheric melting to shallower magmatic pathways.
